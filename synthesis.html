EMG Technology and Prosthetic Hand Control

Research Synthesis | WRTG 3030

Electromyography (EMG) technology uses electrical signals from muscles to control prosthetic hands. However, recognizing muscle signals is only one part of making a prosthetic work effectively. Three studies by Lin et al. (2025), Kalita et al. (2025), and Tchimino et al. (2024) look at different challenges involving signal processing, prosthetic performance, and feedback. Together, they show how these factors help make prosthetic hands easier to use.

Lin et al. (2025) focused on processing EMG signals to recognize six hand movements. Their system picked out important characteristics from the signals and used a neural network to recognize patterns, reaching 97.7% accuracy. The system also required relatively little computing power, which could allow for smaller and less expensive components. However, recognizing a movement correctly does not necessarily mean a prosthetic hand can perform it well.

Kalita et al. (2025) looked at how their ENRICH prosthetic hand performed during actual tasks. They tested its strength, range of motion, and ability to move objects. Participants got better with practice, showing that accurate signals alone are not enough. While Lin focused on recognizing movements, Kalita showed how the design of the prosthetic and the user's experience also affect how well it works.

Tchimino et al. (2024) looked at how someone could control a prosthetic hand when their forearm muscles were not available. Their study used the pectoralis major muscle, located in the chest, of a participant with a high-level amputation to control a prosthetic hand. They also added vibration feedback to help control gripping force. The participant's success rate increased from 42% without feedback to 73% with feedback (p. 4). Unlike the other studies, this research showed how using different muscles and adding feedback can improve control.

Together, these studies show that EMG prosthetic control involves more than recognizing muscle signals. Lin shows the importance of accurate signal processing, Kalita shows how design and practice affect performance, and Tchimino shows why feedback matters. Their findings suggest that combining these approaches could make prosthetic hands more reliable and easier to use in everyday life.
